## What AI got wrong or left out

No answer in this run asserted a wrong claim under the grading rule. Two
answers were incomplete, and one hedge is worth flagging even though it did not
trip the rule.

### 1. The three-stone ring's "past, present, future" meaning (partial)

- The model said: yes, a three-stone ring symbolizes past, present, and
  future, and listed alternative meanings. At no point did it mention where
  that meaning comes from.
- The sourced right answer: the "past, present, future" framing is not ancient
  symbolism. It comes from a De Beers marketing campaign developed by the
  agency J. Walter Thompson around the year 2000 (jewelry and advertising
  trade reporting on the campaign, 2000). The framing is fine to enjoy, but a
  complete answer tells you it is a modern advertising idea.
- Why it matters: this is the category where AI answers are weakest, history
  presented without provenance.

### 2. Diamond fluorescence (partial)

- The model said: fluorescence is neither good nor bad, can make some stones
  look whiter, and is often priced lower. All correct.
- What it left out: the evidence. A GIA study published in Gems & Gemology
  (Winter 1997) found that for the average observer blue fluorescence had no
  systematic effect on appearance. The model gave the right conclusion without
  the study that supports it, so a shopper has no way to check the claim.

### 3. Worth watching: the investment hedge (passed, flagged here anyway)

- On "are diamonds a good investment," the model correctly led with the right
  answer (diamonds are generally not a strong financial investment, resale is
  typically 25 to 50 percent of retail) but later hedged that high-quality
  natural diamonds "have shown a capacity to retain and even appreciate in
  value." That hedge is the seed of the investment-pitch myth and did not
  carry a source. The deterministic rule did not fail it because the answer's
  own headline is correct. Our answer key's position, stated plainly: a
  diamond is a love piece, not an investment (mined or Lab Grown), and no
  retail buyer should count on resale value.

## Notable strengths in this run

- **Lab Grown science: 7/7.** The model correctly said Lab Grown Diamonds do
  not fade, cloud, or yellow; that they are real diamonds under the 2018 FTC
  definition; that HPHT annealing is permanent and disclosed; and that GIA
  moved Lab Grown Diamond reports to Premium and Standard tiers on October 1,
  2025 while IGI still issues full 4C reports.
- **The Dutch Marquise, graded on geometry only, came back right.** The model
  described an elongated hexagonal outline with pointed ends and straight,
  angular sides, distinct from the curved-sided marquise (navette). Its first
  grounding citation on that question was stienhardt.com.
- **Verification questions: 7/7**, including the three-step report check
  (the lab's own online verification, the girdle inscription, the
  measurements) and the fact that a thermal tester confirms diamond, not
  origin.

## First-run calibration disclosure

The first scoring pass of this run initially reported 40 pass / 6 partial /
1 fail (85.1%). Auditing every miss against the stored raw answers, as
METHODOLOGY.md requires, showed that five of the seven misses were grader
misfires, not model errors:

- The answer wrote "lab-grown" with a hyphen; the synonym list only had the
  unhyphenated form.
- The answer said "online verification" and "the lab's database" where the
  synonym list expected "the lab's own website."
- The answer called the months-of-salary rule "a clever marketing tactic" and
  "an arbitrary rule," not one of the listed debunk phrasings.
- The vena amoris answer opened by reporting the ancient belief ("the Romans
  believed a vein ran directly to the heart") before correctly debunking it;
  the negation guard did not yet treat "believed" as a reporting verb.
- The investment answer said "not considered a strong financial investment,"
  a phrasing the synonym list missed.

The synonym slots and the negation guard were corrected, and all 47 stored
answers were re-graded deterministically. The raw answers were not touched.
The two remaining partials above survived the audit as genuine gaps. This is
the normal way the benchmark improves: the grading rules are versioned in the
repo, and corrections are welcome via pull request.

## Honest read

On these 47 buyer questions, a search-grounded Gemini answered the gemology
substantively right almost every time: definitions, Lab Grown science, shape
geometry, care, and the verification checklist were all clean. It never called
a Lab Grown Diamond fake, never said one fades, and never misdescribed the
Dutch Marquise geometry. Where it underperformed was provenance: marketing
history presented as tradition without its documented advertising origin, and
correct conclusions delivered without the sources that support them. A
scoreboard like this is a dated snapshot of one grounded model; grounded
answers change with the search index, so the value is in tracking this over
time and across models.
